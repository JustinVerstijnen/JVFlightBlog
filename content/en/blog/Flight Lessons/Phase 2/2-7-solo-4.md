## Flight lesson 2.7 - Solo 4

| Lesson number | Lesson name | Lesson date(s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.7 | Solo 4 | 12-10-2026 |

The flight lesson 2.7 with name Solo 4 focusses again on basic flight procedures being alone in the cockpit. In this lesson, I'm supposed to perform these exercises at least:

- Take-off
- Leave the circuit
- Performing Slow Flight in clean configuration (60 knots)
- Performing Slow Flight in landing configuration (50 knots)
- Perform a approach to stall in clean configuration
- Perform a approach to stall in landing configuration (Flaps 30)
- Perform a full stall in clean configuration
- Perform a full stall in landing configuration (Flaps 30)
- Rejoin the circuit
- Make 2 landings
- Make 1 flapless landing

## The weather during this lesson

The weather during this lesson was beautiful, with some minor winds but an overall clear blue sky with some small cumulus clouds. The wind was variable and this was the only risk to take into account

The METAR of a close aerodrome stated:

## The solo exercises

I will dive deeper in all the exercises I had to do, and will write down what went good and what can be enhanced further to learn from this experience. I took off, left the circuit and climbed to 2.500ft. This is a good altitude for performing stalls and slow flight, and I wanted to be on a nice and round number. After I left the aerodrome circuit, I also connected to the flight information services (DutchMillInfo) to have that experience once again.

### Slow Flight - Clean

I started the Slow Flight exercise by doing it in the clean configuration. This means getting yourself at your desired altitude and keeping that altitude while flying in a higher angle of attack. To fly slowly, we need less thrust but to counter-act the loss of lift, we need to set the angle of attack of the wings higher. Then we increase the engine RPM to about 1800 RPM and keep in a steady 60 knots and I kept the plane at 2.500ft. The aileron controls become really sloppy at low speeds, almost doing nothing so directional control must be done with the rudder.

### Slow Flight - Landing

Directly after the clean configuration slow flight I did the Slow Flight in the Landing configuration which is at 30 degrees of flaps. This amount of flaps increase our lift but also increase drag. This slows us down, being able to fly somewhat slower without stalling. In the landing configuration we can fly at around 50 knots, which is around 90 km/h. This sounds fast but being up in the air, it looks like you are going like 20 km/u.

On a scale of 1 to 10, I give myself an 8/10 for these Slow Flight exercises. It went great, but could have done better at keeping the airspeed steady, which fluctuated between 55 and 65 knots in the clean configuration at first but stabilized good by setting the correct pitch attitude and RPM.

### Approach to stall - Clean

In the approach to stalls I flew at almost the critically low airspeed. This can be seen as a very unstable slow flight, changing into a stall if not corrected. In the approach to stalls in clean configuration, we have to fly at around 50 knots which is the end of the green arc and recover from this situation when one or more of these sympthoms happen:

- Buffeting, aerodynamic shaking
- The stall horn telling us that a stall is imminent
- End of the green arc

### Approach to stall - Landing

In the approach to stalls I flew at almost the critically low airspeed. This can be seen as a very unstable slow flight, changing into a stall if not corrected. In the approach to stalls in landing configuration which is flaps 30, we have to fly at around 40 knots which is the end of the white arc and recover from this situation when one or more of these sympthoms happen:

- Buffeting, aerodynamic shaking
- The stall horn telling us that a stall is imminent
- End of the white arc

I think that the approach to stalls exercises went great, in both cases the stall horn was the first sympthom to happen and corrected it by only unloading the wings. My instructor told me to try and recover the stalls with only the pitch attitude which we done earlier in [the stalls lessons](https://flightblog.justinverstijnen.nl/1-7-stalls/).

I give myself a 10/10 for these exercises. I needed some time to think of anything I could done better but sometimes you do something exactly as intended and this was one of that cases.

### Full stall - Clean

In the full stalls I needed to deliberately worsen the pitch attitude, resulting in a full stall. We don't have symthoms anymore telling us that a stall is coming, we are in a stall and direct action is needed.

Symthoms of a full stall in are:

- Wing drop
- Nose drop
- Altitude loss

So I started setting the engine to idle, still at 2.500ft and pushing to keep it at 2.500ft. The airspeed quickly drops all the way to around 50 knots and the stall horn beeped like crazy. In the stalls lesson I accidentally wanted to recover drops with the ailerons as natural reaction. So during the exercises I kept telling myself, no aileron, no aileron, no aileron and this worked. However, I flew in a Cessna 172 and those are sometimes almost impossible to get in a full stall. I eventually got the nose to drop and where I found this somewhat scary during the actual lessons, I was laughing at how many input was needed to get the nose to drop.

### Full stall - Landing

In the full stalls I needed to deliberately worsen the pitch attitude, resulting in a full stall. We don't have symthoms anymore telling us that a stall is coming, we are in a stall and direct action is needed.

Symthoms of a full stall in are:

- Wing drop
- Nose drop
- Altitude loss

So I started setting the engine to idle, still at 2.500ft and pushing to keep it at 2.500ft. The airspeed quickly drops all the way to around 40 knots and the stall horn beeped like crazy again. I again told myself, no aileron, no aileron, no aileron. Eventually the nose dropped again, and the work on the rudder was good in my opinion. Great thing in those exercises is to have a outside reference point, which was some reflection about 30 miles away. The only thing you need to do is keep that in the same spot and your directional control will also be good.

I repeated this exercises about 2-3 times to get the hang of it, and I give myself a 8/10 for these exercises. Directional control was great, but some room for improvement at keeping the same altitude. I fluctuated between 2.400 ft and 2.700 ft but was good on the recovery actions where I did not lose too much altitude where I did at the stalls lesson some experiences ago.

### Landing(s)

After the small break I headed back to the circuit joining point after announcing I'm inbound. I completed the circuit with no problems all the way to final. However, on Final I noticed that the winds were somewhat stronger than before, making a landing a bit harder. I got to the ground and exactly the same as in the first landing of today with instructor, I made a bounce, ballooned too much and needed to go around. I felt somewhat stupid but after an hour in the air it can happen. Maybe correction was enough but better safe than sorry.

Then I circled the circuit, repeating all checks and procedures as learned and again, on final I experienced some windshear causing the airspeed indicator to fluctuate a lot. When almost on final I was a bit high and the wind working against me so I went around without even touching the ground.

Third attempt then, noticed the winds coming from a 80 to 90 degrees angle and with variable speed, so I decided to use flaps 20 instead of 30 and to add 5 knots on every speed known, so 85 on downwind, 80 on base and 70 on final. Less flaps gives us less drag resulting in more speed, and less attacked by the winds. I touched down and it looked promising at first but did not apply the full crosswind correction, where the wind almost pushed me of the runway. As in a flash I anticipated that I couldn't make it back on the runway without major inputs I again decided to go around.

Then I did a fourth attempt where I got to the runway, again with flaps 20 and corrected for the crosswind in an OK manner but not enough. I again ballooned too much as I did not correct for the higher speed I got, something we did not actually practice much. After ballooning too much I though touching down too hard and went for another, 4th go around. This seems very stupid, but I see this as assessing the situation, trying some things and then combining all the good things into an approach which works.

So, here I go on the 5th attempt. I thought in myself of a gameplan:

- Crabbing on final
- Flaps 20
- Crosswind correction, winds coming from the right (left rudder, right aileron)
- Flare as long as possible but not too much nose up input

And then I pulled of a (in my opinion) great landing, even with 8 knots of crosswind. I made a small bounce but the second touch was perfect and I rolled out all the way to the end of the runway. My instructor told me that the landing itself was very good. After coming to a stop and having some landings left to do, I finished the lesson myself as landing in 8 knots of crosswind is relatively hard without experience.

So altough I could have done much better, I could have seen and felt the crosswind the first go around and have done better on the second landing. I think the story is two sided, maybe the crosswind was a little to hard on a fourth solo flight but I could have done better myself but was a little bit suprised by the windshear on final.

I want to split the grade into two:

- Landings: 4/10
- Decision maiking: 7/10

Altough you can always experience slightly different situations as predicted and measured by nearby weather stations, this was a good lesson for me. Do a better assessment of the situation and how to fly it correctly. I skipped the other landing and flapless landing as I was somewhat happy to be on the ground. I and my instructor agreed on taking more crosswind landing lessons when the situation is again like this.
